% traditional rag
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG, \cite{lewis2020retrieval}) models are designed to generate text that is grounded in real-world factual knowlege. They have been widely adopted for knowledge-intensive NLP tasks \cite{shao2025reasonir} such as long-form question answering \cite{xia2024ground}, scientific question answering \cite{lala2023paperqa} and question synthesis \cite{xu2024kiwi}, generation with sentence-level citations \cite{zhang2024longcite, xia2024ground} and academic text generation \cite{asai2024openscholar, wang2025scholarcopilot}. RAG models usually consist of a generator, a pre-trained sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) model (e.g. a Transformer-based \cite{vaswani2017attention} decoder, see \refsec{related-work}), and a retriever with access to a retrieval corpus, which may be implemented as a dense vector index (e.g. a HNSW \cite{malkov2018efficient} index, see \refsec{hnsw}) \cite{lewis2020retrieval}. Thus they combine pre-trained paramentric memory with non-paramentric (retrieval-based) memory \cite{lewis2020retrieval}. 

The RAG architecture \cite{lewis2020retrieval} is comprised of a retriever $p_\eta$ consisting of a query encoder $q$ and a retrieval corpus $d$, and a generator $p_\theta$ (see \reffig{fig:rag_architecture}). Given a query $x$, the retriever $p_\eta(z|x)$ (with parameters $\eta$) computes a distribution over the text documents $z$ in the retrieval corpus $d$ according to $x$ \cite{lewis2020retrieval}. This distribution is used to obtain the top-k most relevant documents with respect to the query. The generator $p_\theta(y_i|x,z,y_{1:i-1})$, parametrized by $\theta$, then predicts each next token $y_i$ based on the previous tokens $y_{1:i-1}$, the given query $x$ and a document $z$ from the top-k retrieved documents (according to $p_\eta$) \cite{lewis2020retrieval}. Lewis et al. base their retriever on the \mono{Dense Passage Retriever} \cite{karpukhin2020dense} and use a pre-trained \mono{BART-large} \cite{lewis2019bart} as the generator.
